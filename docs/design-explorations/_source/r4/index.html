Wasel: 20 schools, 20 structures

Each school was researched on its own (one search each, source linked on its card). The brief on each card is written from what the school actually believes, then turned into the structure of the product: a command sheet, a shelf, a storyboard, a ledger, a room that is silent unless something is wrong. All twenty use the same nine orders. Click Enlarge on a card to see it at full size.

Three of them (Wabi-sabi, Hashiya, Hurufiyya) lean on Arabic and Tunisian material; none has been checked by a native reader. Several are not design schools in the strict sense (triage, accounting, library science, aviation): they are institutions whose philosophy of information is so clear it can drive a product. Each card says so.

01

Bauhaus

Art and design school · Gropius, Kandinsky, Moholy-Nagy, Bayer · source

What the school believes

Form follows function, and the smallest visual alphabet is the triangle, square and circle in the three primaries. Kandinsky's expected answer: yellow triangle, red square, blue circle. Bayer set text in lowercase only, to reduce the alphabet to its essential geometry.

So the product becomes

Wasel already marks tiers with a circle, a diamond and a triangle, so the tiers become the Bauhaus forms: a red square for high risk, a yellow triangle for medium, a blue circle for low. The page is three bands, one per form, on an asymmetric layout with a diagonal bar.

02

Constructivism

Art and design movement · Rodchenko, Lissitzky · source

What the school believes

Art as an instrument for social purpose, not decoration. Text is a structural element and strong diagonals imply motion and urgency. Posters used strident slogans and high-contrast composition to be understood instantly by anyone.

So the product becomes

The product speaks in commands. The page is four slanted bars, each an imperative: CONFIRM the address, PREPAY first, CHECK quickly, SHIP now, with the names under it and a count set huge. There is no list of orders at all, only what to do.

03

Wabi-sabi and kintsugi

Japanese philosophy · Imperfection, impermanence, incompleteness · source

What the school believes

Nothing lasts, nothing is finished, nothing is perfect. Kintsugi mends broken pottery with gold and does not hide the fracture: breakage and repair are part of the object's history.

So the product becomes

Orders are vessels on a shelf. The longer one waits, the more it weathers; high-risk ones carry visible cracks, one per reason. When the customer's reply resolves a reason, the crack is filled with gold and stays visible, so the history of the order is the surface of the order.

04

Saul Bass

Graphic design and film titles · Saul Bass · source

What the school believes

The problem defines the solution. A title sequence is the first act of the film and sets its tone; find a metaphor, not just the prettiest frame. Distil a whole story into a simple, angular graphic landscape.

So the product becomes

Every order is a three-frame strip of film: what they said, how it will end (a cut-paper shape with the score), and what you do (an arrow and a verb). Strips stack like a reel. The unit of the product is the story of one order.

05

Metro, Windows Phone

Interface design language · Microsoft Design Language 2 · source

What the school believes

Four principles: typography, motion, content not chrome, and honesty. Based on classic Swiss graphic design and influenced by public transport signs; flat colour live tiles replace icons.

So the product becomes

A horizontal panorama of sections (orders, messages, results) whose huge light titles run off the screen edge, hinting at the next section. Each section is a hub of flat live tiles: a big '4 need you', a tile that rotates through the customer's quote. Content is the interface; there are no borders.

06

Newsroom: the inverted pyramid

Journalistic practice · Wire-service news writing · source

What the school believes

Put the most critical facts first and the rest in descending order of importance, judged by relative news value. It respects reader time, supports skimming and lets an editor cut from the bottom.

So the product becomes

A front page ranked by news value. The riskiest order is the lead story with a headline and a lede (who, what, why); the next are briefs; the low-risk ones are one line at the bottom. You act as editor: Run it (send the question), Hold, or Spike (cancel).

Field triage (START)

Emergency medicine system · Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment · source

What the school believes

Greatest good for the greatest number. Categorise, do not diagnose: three quick checks in under a minute and a colour tag (red immediate, yellow delayed, green minor, black expectant) that decides who gets resources first.

So the product becomes

The screen is a field with four coloured zones and orders hung as triage tags. Each tag has three checks (address, value, history) and a tear-off strip with the action. Sorting is by rule, not by browsing: you work the red zone first.

08

Airport signage (Frutiger and Aicher)

Wayfinding design · Adrian Frutiger, Otl Aicher · source

What the school believes

Signs must be legible at extreme angles, in peripheral vision, from moving walkways. Frutiger blurred letterforms to test them; a character had to have the same clarity as an arrow.

So the product becomes

A departures board for orders: each row is a destination (the next action), an order code, a pictogram and a large arrow: confirm, prepay, quick check, ship. Direction is the main information; the seller follows arrows, not paragraphs.

09

Pacioli's ledger

Accounting tradition · Luca Pacioli, 1494 · source

What the school believes

Every debit has a twin credit and they always go together; the sum of debits equals the sum of credits. The system lets a merchant reconcile every transaction, find errors and settle disputes.

So the product becomes

Each order is a T-account. The left side lists what raises the risk (debits), the right what lowers it (credits), and the balance is the score. A journal records each entry in order. A customer reply is a new credit entry that moves the balance, 87 to 16, and the book still balances.

Cybersyn and management by exception

Cybernetic management room · Stafford Beer, Gui Bonsiepe · source

What the school believes

Four levels of control with algedonic feedback: if one level does not fix a problem in time, the next level up is told. Decision-making is devolved so the room only speaks when something leaves its limits.

So the product becomes

A bright, almost empty room. A single ring says 7 of 9 orders are within limits, and only the two that were escalated to you appear, each showing what the assistant already tried. A footer shows the escalation chain: customer, assistant, you.

Macintosh and direct manipulation

Interface design · Susan Kare, Bill Atkinson, Apple · source

What the school believes

A computer anyone could use without a manual, friendly rather than technical. Icons are economy of expression: more like road signs than illustrations. The desktop metaphor lets you act on things directly.

So the product becomes

A desktop. Orders are document icons in a window; the decisions are folders you drag them onto (ask for the address, ask for prepayment, ship) and a cancel bin. Deciding is a physical act, not a button inside a form.

Hashiya: the commentary page

Arabic manuscript tradition · Marginal glosses and commentary · source

What the school believes

For centuries readers wrote in the margins and between lines, explaining, criticising and supplementing the main text. The margin is a productive space of interpretation, not a periphery.

So the product becomes

The customer's own message is the central text, set in a ruled frame in Arabic script and Arabizi. The reasons are glosses around it, each pointing at the exact phrase with the points it added, plus a gloss that translates Darija into plain words. The product is a close reading.

Hurufiyya and the calligram

Arab modernist art movement · Madiha Omar, Nja Mahdaoui · source

What the school believes

Artists stripped letters down to their visual elements and used the gesture of the calligrapher within the language of modern art, building a culture-specific visual language. Mahdaoui, the 'choreographer of letters', is Tunisian.

So the product becomes

The order is a composition. The customer's words are set large and flowing along curves; the phrases that raised the risk are in heavy black ink and the rest in pale grey, so risk is literally ink density. The score is a round red seal in the corner, and a column of small seals indexes the orders.

Imhof's terrain

Cartography · Eduard Imhof, Swiss topographic mapping · source

What the school believes

Show the terrain as a three-dimensional surface with shaded relief and contour lines, oblique light from the upper left, and subtle colour that rises with elevation. Only simplicity provides a lasting impression.

So the product becomes

Risk becomes a landscape. One axis is how clear the address is, the other is order value; the height of the ground is the risk, drawn with contour lines every 20 points and a hypsometric tint. Orders are survey points with a spot height (Sami, 87 m). The summit is where a vague address meets a high value.

The IKEA manual

Democratic design · Gillis Lundgren, flat-pack instructions · source

What the school believes

Instructions understood regardless of language, culture or experience, using line drawings. Every step is small enough that no single action overwhelms, even when the whole is complex.

So the product becomes

Each order is handled like an assembly manual: step 1 read what he wrote, step 2 the missing part (a full address, shown as the parts bag), step 3 send the question and wait. Wordless line drawings, one step highlighted, and a tick when a part arrives.

Ranganathan's facets

Library science · S. R. Ranganathan, Colon Classification · source

What the school believes

Hierarchical schemes are too limiting, because an item can belong to more than one subject. Describe it by facets: personality, matter, energy, space and time. Search can move along multiple paths.

So the product becomes

Browsing by facets instead of one list: personality (new or returning customer), matter (the goods), energy (what to do), space (rural or urban), time (how long waiting). Every order carries a short class mark such as N:H:C:R:3h, and your current filters read as one chain of colon-joined chips.

17

The aviation checklist

Human factors · Boeing Model 299, Gawande · source

What the school believes

Complexity exceeds any one person's ability to get everything right under pressure, so skilled people overlook the obvious. A short checklist, small enough to fit on an index card, made the Flying Fortress flyable.

So the product becomes

Before shipping, each order has a checklist card: message read, address complete, value within limit, prior deliveries, phone given. Wasel fills in what it can; a pause line forbids handing over to the courier until the address is complete, and ABORT is a red button.

Chiaroscuro

Painting technique · Caravaggio, Rembrandt · source

What the school believes

Light directs attention. A single dominant light source outside the frame and strong contrast guide the eye and build urgency, as in Caravaggio's The Calling of Saint Matthew.

So the product becomes

The room is dark and one light falls from the upper left. Hierarchy is brightness, not size or colour: a score of 89 sits in the light and a score of 9 is in half shadow, all rows the same size. The lit order opens beside the list with its risky phrases picked out in gold.

Zellige: the finished panel

Maghrebi craft · Maâlems of Fez · source

What the school believes

Thousands of hand-cut glazed tesserae assembled from a limited vocabulary of shapes into a pattern that comes right when finished. The craft is passed from master to apprentice.

Today is one panel of nine octagonal tiles. A tile is bare biscuit until you decide the order, then it is glazed in its tier colour. The day is finished when no tile is bare. The reward for clearing the work is a completed pattern, not an empty inbox.

Marey's graphical timetable

Information graphics · Ibry, popularised by É.-J. Marey · source

What the school believes

Time on one axis and the route on the other: the slope of each line shows where each train is headed, how fast and where it pauses. An entire schedule can be understood in a glance; Tufte put it on the cover of his book.

So the product becomes

A time-space chart of the day. Stages run down the side, hours run across, and every order is a line. Steep means fast; a flat dotted line means it is stalled waiting for the customer. Four red lines have been flat for over two hours.
